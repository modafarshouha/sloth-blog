Debates about AI safety can sound existential, yet much of what happens in practice comes down to incentives. When big-tech leaders talk about "safety", they often hide other motives beneath it, such as: reputational damage, fines, lawsuits, or maybe development interruptions. The harder question is not whether a model *could* cause harm, but who is held responsible when it does, and whether the consequences are serious enough to change behavior. This is called the "responsibility gap"[^1]: when usual rules for accountability are not enough to deal with systems that act in ways their creators cannot fully predict.

## Safety talk, spreadsheet logic

I do not think most participants in the AI safety conversation are lying. They may genuinely worry about misuse, bias, or long-run risk. Still, the visible behavior of the industry often looks like a cost-benefit calculation. If the expected price of misbehaving (in the safety sense) stays manageable, development tends to continue. In other words, the drive is not moral; it is the open-market competition gain.

Big-tech firms will keep developing more capable models because, in a capitalist environment, their primary drive is profit. A "good" corporation is one that profits[^2]. Safety becomes an item: worth funding when it protects the brand, easy to ignore when it does not.

## But... We are afraid of China

When criticized for this profit-first approach, corporations often justify a wild, uncontrolled push for AI development by weaponizing geopolitical fear. But one could argue that a model built under tight government oversight is *likely* more safe. Public institutions have different incentives than one built to win the next benchmark race. They are not saints, and the "public sector" does not mean "aligned with your values". But they are not optimized for profit alone. Profit is not their *only* drive.

That makes a state (even the one you trust least) a somewhat more controllable entity than a tech giant. Corporations are designed to compete; states are designed (however badly) to persist and to manage collective risk.

## Nuclear weapons, MAD, and big tech

The nuclear comparison is overused and has its flaws[^3], but it highlights a fundamental difference between state and corporate incentives[^4]. A handful of states hold nuclear weapons; some for military use, yet we have not seen nuclear war (*and I hope this sentence does not jinx that*). MAD (Mutually Assured Destruction) is a depressing equilibrium, but it is an equilibrium: the cost of misbehavior is civilization-scale, and everyone at the table knows it. What keeps a frontier AI lab in check the same way? Expert controls alone, and voluntary safety pledges on a blog post are definitely not enough!

## Back to the gap

So when we argue about AI safety, I think a good point to start from is the responsibility gap: when an AI does something harmful, who pays? Who gets sued, regulated, or politically ruined? And if the answer is "nobody, really", or "only after the damage is already public", then safety talk is mostly theater.

That may sound radical. It might also be obvious. Until accountability is real (until the cost of misbehavior is high enough that skipping safety is bad for business) we should expect more powerful models, faster releases, and louder assurances. The check is not a benchmark. The check is consequences.
