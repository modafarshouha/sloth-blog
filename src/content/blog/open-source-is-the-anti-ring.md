*Part 2 of a 3-part series exploring AI through Middle-earth.*

Most of us these days use models we cannot inspect, and these models live on servers we cannot control. We have little say over who can access our data and how it is processed.

When you run an open model on your own machine, your prompt never leaves the room. However, a model small enough for a laptop is usually less capable than the hosted ones, and many "open" models are in practice run on someone else's server anyway.

Still, the difference in who holds the power is real, and it is the part worth thinking about.

## Towers and seeing-stones

In *The Two Towers*, the story moves from one person's burden to a clash of powers, centred on two towers: Saruman's and Sauron's. Both are tall, central places where power is gathered. Their rulers also have palantíri, seeing-stones that let their user see far away, but that also let someone else look back at *them*[^1].

Centralized AI is similar: it is powerful and useful, but it is a single point of control, since someone decides what the model may say and when your access ends. It is convenient, but it only works as long as you trust the provider.

## The Ring is about concentration

The One Ring is a tool for concentrating power in one hand. When Gandalf is offered it, he refuses to take it even to use it for good, because the good he would try to do with it would turn to evil[^1]. The same worry applies to a few companies holding the most capable AI.

An open model is the opposite design. Researchers who studied open foundation models describe more competition and transparency, and decision-making power spread across many hands rather than a few[^2]. Open models can also carry risks, since misuse is easier when anyone can modify a model[^2]. Still, I would argue that centralised power is riskier: if one hand misuses it, everyone else is exposed and has nowhere else to turn.

## It is not all shiny

"Open" has, to some extent, become a marketing word. By the Open Source Initiative's definition, many "open" models are not *open*: they release their weights, but not enough about the data and code for a skilled person to build a substantially equivalent model[^3]. A lot of "open" is just "downloadable".

There is also a less comfortable argument: if building or running large models at scale still depends on resources that only a few companies have, the control remains with them, however open the model is[^4]. The Ring stays in the same hands.

## One Ring, many hands

Open is a direction, not a switch; releasing the weights is only one step. It is also worth asking what you depend on: if a tool disappears or changes tomorrow, what happens to your work? And you do not have to go fully open or avoid hosted tools altogether. This Ring is not going to be destroyed, so the realistic aim is that no single hand holds all of it.

In the last part, I look at what ordinary people can do about all this.
